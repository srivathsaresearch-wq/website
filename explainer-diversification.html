Diversification,without the jargon.

“Don’t put all your eggs in one basket” is the idea. Doing it well means knowing which baskets tend to fall together.

4 min read · Updated 17 October 2026

The idea Different ways to spread money When it looks diversified but isn't What it can't do Questions to ask yourself

In short

  • Diversification spreads money across things that don't all rise and fall at the same time.
  • Owning many things that behave alike isn't real diversification.
  • It reduces the damage from any one bad outcome. It doesn't remove risk or guarantee a gain.

The idea

If all your savings are in one company's shares and that company runs into trouble, your whole plan suffers. If your money is spread across many businesses, one bad outcome hurts far less. That is diversification: making sure no single thing can sink you.

Different ways to spread money

  • Across companies: many businesses rather than a handful.
  • Across industries: banks, technology, consumer goods, healthcare and so on, because a whole industry can have a bad few years.
  • Across types of asset: equity, debt, gold and cash each behave differently (flip through equity, debt and gold).
  • Across time: investing in instalments rather than all on one day (see SIP or lump sum).

When it looks diversified but isn't

Owning five mutual funds can feel diversified. But if all five hold mostly the same large companies, you own the same thing five times, with five sets of costs. Ten shares from the same industry can all fall together when that industry hits a rough patch.

The test isn't how many things you own. It is whether they are likely to fall at the same time, for the same reason. Look at what is inside: funds publish a regular list of what they hold.

What it can't do

When markets fall sharply, many things fall together for a while. Diversification softens the blow; it doesn't stop it.

It also means you will always own something doing worse than the best performer. That is the point, not a flaw (see why chasing last year's winner often disappoints).

Questions to ask yourself

  • If my biggest holding halved, how much of my total would I lose?
  • Do my funds hold mostly the same companies?
  • Is my money spread across more than one type of asset?
  • Do I have cash set aside for the near term?
