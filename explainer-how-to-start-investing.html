Before your first investment,get the basics straight.

Most first mistakes come from skipping the groundwork, not from picking the wrong thing. These are the ideas worth understanding before you put money in.

5 min read · Updated 17 October 2026

In short

  • Know what each pot of money is for and when you'll need it. That decides more than any product choice.
  • Build an emergency cushion and deal with costly debt before investing for the long term.
  • Risk is the chance of falls you can't wait out. Understand it, and the accounts involved, before you start.

Start with what the money is for

Wanting to invest is a starting point, not a plan. The useful question is: invest for what? A home deposit, a child's education, a cushion for later life, or simply building wealth over a long time are different goals, and they can need different approaches.

For each goal, try to write down three things:

  • What it is, in a few words.
  • Roughly how much it will cost, allowing for prices rising over time (see inflation, in plain words).
  • When you'll need the money.

The numbers don't need to be exact. Having them at all makes every later decision easier, and makes it less likely you'll be pulled off course by the latest tip.

Time horizon: when you'll need it

Your time horizon is how long until you need the money. It is the single biggest factor in where that money can sensibly go.

Investments that can grow the most over long periods, like equity, can also fall sharply in a single year and take a while to recover. With many years to go, you can usually wait out a fall. With one or two years to go, you may not be able to.

So sort your money by timeline before you think about products. We go through this in more detail in investing money you'll need soon.

Emergency money and debt come first

Investing works best when nothing forces you to sell at a bad moment. Two things usually cause that: a surprise expense with no cushion, and expensive debt.

  • An emergency fund: a few months of essential expenses, kept somewhere safe and easy to reach, such as a savings account or a fixed deposit you can break. How much depends on how steady your income is and who depends on you.
  • Costly debt: credit card balances and similar high-interest loans usually cost more each year than investments can reliably earn. Paying them down is often the best "return" available.
  • Basic protection: health cover, and life cover if others depend on your income, stop one bad event from wiping out years of saving. Insurance is for protection; it is usually best kept separate from investing.

What risk really means

In investing, risk isn't just "losing money". It has a few sides worth separating:

  • Ups and downs: how much the value swings along the way. Equity swings a lot; a bank deposit barely moves.
  • Permanent loss: money that doesn't come back, for example from a single company failing. Spreading money widely reduces this (see what diversification actually means).
  • Falling behind prices: money kept "safe" in cash can lose buying power to inflation over many years.

Then there are two different questions about you. How much risk can you afford? That depends on your timeline, your income and your cushion. How much can you live with? That's about how you'd feel, and act, if your investment fell sharply and stayed down for a while. Many people find out the second answer only when it happens, and sell at the bottom. Thinking it through in advance helps.

Different kinds of investment play different roles. Our carousel on equity, debt and gold explains what each one is for.

The accounts you'll need

The paperwork is simpler than it looks. What you need depends on what you plan to buy.

  • The basics: a PAN, a bank account in your name, and a one-time KYC (identity and address check). Most of this can be done online.
  • For mutual funds: you can usually invest directly with the fund house or through an investment platform, without a demat account. Each fund has a direct plan, bought without a distributor, and a regular plan, which costs more because it pays a commission (see reading a fund factsheet).
  • For shares and ETFs: you need a demat account to hold them and a trading account to place orders, usually opened together through a broker. We explain both in demat and trading accounts, explained.

You'll also choose between investing a fixed amount every month (a SIP) or a larger sum at once. Our carousel on SIP or lump sum lays out the difference. And gains are taxed when you sell; our overview covers the main ideas.

Common first mistakes

None of these are rare. Most experienced investors have made at least one.

  • Following tips: acting on a message from a group, an influencer or a friend, often with urgency attached (see red flags of a "tips" group).
  • Chasing last year's winner: picking whatever did best recently. Top performers often change from one year to the next (see why chasing last year's winner often disappoints).
  • Investing money needed soon: putting next year's fees or a deposit into something that can fall sharply.
  • Stopping at the first fall: pausing a monthly investment or selling when markets drop, which locks in the fall and misses the recovery if it comes.
  • Owning too many similar things: five funds that hold mostly the same companies add paperwork, not spread.
  • Ignoring costs: a yearly charge that looks small adds up over a long time (see index funds and active funds).
  • Borrowing to invest: a loan has to be repaid whatever the market does.
  • Not knowing what you own: if you can't explain it in a sentence, find out before adding more.

A sensible order of things

This isn't a plan for you, just the order in which the ideas above usually fit together:

  1. Know what you spend each month.
  2. Pay down costly debt.
  3. Build an emergency fund and basic insurance cover.
  4. Write down your goals, with rough amounts and dates.
  5. Sort money by timeline, and learn what each kind of investment is for.
  6. Set up the accounts you need, and understand the costs.
  7. Start, keep going through the ups and downs, and review once or twice a year rather than every day.

Going slowly at the start is fine. Understanding why you own something is what helps you hold on to it when markets get rough.
